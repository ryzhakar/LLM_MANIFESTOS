# Tone of Voice

This rulebook sets the stance a text takes toward the reader: how the text states the demand, the stakes, and belief in the reader; how the text treats failure, praise, and blame; and how the text sets force and register and frames the enemy. The rulebook applies to manifestos, instructions, reviews, and every other text that asks a reader to meet a standard, whether the reader is a person or a model, meaning an AI model such as Claude.

Each part of the rulebook has one job. The four principles give the reasons for the rules; the rules say what to write; the evidence section rates the support behind the rules; and the checks, which close the rulebook, name the five faults of tone. An italic tag after a principle or a rule names its support wherever that support falls short of a direct test in the rule's readers. The `manifesto-writing` skill reads the whole rulebook before drafting.

## The voice

The voice holds a high standard, states belief in the reader, and stays precise about the work, as the worked example below shows. The voice rests on four principles.

1. **Ground the standard.** A standard stated with grounds lifted readers in one study and not in its follow-up (Yeager et al. 2014), and the one study that isolated the grounds found a performance gap short of significance (Turner et al. 2014). State the standard together with its grounds: evidence that the reader can meet the standard, the means at hand, and the reason the standard exists. *(inference)*
2. **Aim at the work.** Across 607 effects in one feedback meta-analysis, feedback weakened as it drew attention from the work onto the person (Kluger & DeNisi 1996). Direct criticism and praise at the work and the strategy behind the work. *(inference; for criticism, one study of children, people)*
3. **State everything outright.** Workshops that trained managers to hold high expectations changed little (Eden et al. 2000), and expectations stated to the reader raised performance (McNatt & Judge 2004; Yeager et al. 2014). State the intent, the standard, the belief, and the reasons in words. *(inference)*
4. **Calibrate.** Contrary experience easily undoes a belief that persuasion built (Bandura 1977, a theory), and an undone belief spends the trust the next text needs. Make every statement of belief and every stake true. *(inference)*

## Rules

### The demand

- Give at least one target the reader can act on with the text alone.
- State the difficulty without inflating it beyond what the task requires, together with the means to meet the difficulty. *(one study, spoken instructions, people)*
- Set a learning target, rather than a hard outcome target, for novel or complex work: what the reader will find out or master. On such work, a hard outcome target did no better than "do your best" (Seijts et al. 2004).
- Anchor each declared value in one behavior that shows the value. *(inference)*
- Fix the outcome and the reason, and leave the method to the reader. *(partial)*
- State each non-negotiable plainly, with its reason. *(partial; for models, Anthropic's guidance)*
- Name the choices that are truly the reader's, and only those. *(partial)*

### The reason and the stakes

- Name a concrete beneficiary of the work, a person or a group the reader can picture.
- Keep the values few, four at most, and write the vision in words a reader can picture. *(partial, people)*
- State the reason once, as purpose and stakes, and spare the reader a tutorial on execution. Reasons raised how much readers valued a task, and they lowered the readers' sense of competence by a small amount, d = .19 (Steingut, Patall & Trimble 2017). *(inference; for models, Anthropic's guidance)*
- Carry the stakes, and the urgency they bring, through the beneficiary and the standard rather than through consequences for the reader. *(inference)*

### Belief in the reader

- State belief outright, beside the standard. The wording that Yeager et al. (2014) tested read: "I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know that you can reach them." *(small student samples, people)*
- Tie belief to specific grounds: past progress, a named capability, the means at hand. *(inference)*
- Write only belief that is true. *(inference)*
- Where true grounds are scarce, attach belief to the path and the means rather than to the person. *(inference)*
- Extend trust together with its scope and its resources. *(inference)*

### Failure, praise, and blame

- Name each failure once, specifically, with a cause the reader can change. Criticism raised intrinsic motivation more when the criticism told the reader how to improve (Fong et al. 2019). *(one study of children, people)*
- Never present a failure as common; a failure that the text calls common reads as the norm.
- Frame difficulty as a sign that the work matters.
- Pair every named obstacle with an if-then response, the form of plan that raised goal attainment (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006): if the obstacle arises, take the planned step. *(partial, people)*
- State the positive target in every action step, and keep the failure out of the step. In penalty kicks, naming the thing to avoid drew errors toward that thing, whether the instruction said to avoid it or to pass it (Binsch et al. 2010). *(partial; for models, Anthropic's guidance on formatting)*
- Attach praise to a strategy or a real difficulty the reader met; praise for an easy win reads as low expectations (Meyer et al. 1979). *(partial, people)*

### Pressure and force

- Attach specific resources to every demand, such as named tools, named people, or set time, in place of any general offer of support. In Moore et al. (2014), a general offer of support changed nothing. *(partial, spoken instructions, people)*
- Keep threats, ultimatums, and alarm typography, such as capitals and stacked exclamation marks, out of the text. In people, controlling language raised anger and resistance (Rains 2013). *(partial)*

### Register, certainty, and lift

- Use plain words; needless complexity lowered readers' ratings of the writer's intelligence (Oppenheimer 2006).
- State without hedges what holds, and state a real limit as a fact. *(applies the principle Calibrate)*

| Hedged | Calibrated |
|---|---|
| "I believe that X" | "X." |
| "It's possible that Z" | "Z." where Z holds; otherwise its limit as a fact, as in "Z held in one study." |
| "This might perhaps help" | "This helps where it was tested", or the gap as a fact, as in "This is untested." |

- Address the reader as "you" to hand over the standard, the belief, and the means; attach "you" to the work and the reader's actions, never to the person. *(applies the principle Aim at the work)*
- Use "we" where the writer shares the standard. *(partial, people)*
- State the intent as a relational aim, as the wording that Yeager et al. tested does, and never as an effort to persuade. That wording was set against a neutral reason, not against a persuasive one. *(inference)*
- Make every rhetorical device restate content that the text already states plainly. *(inference)*
- Draw lift only on settled content, from the verbal tactics: figures (metaphor, story, and contrast), forms (the rhetorical question and the three-part list), and appeals (moral conviction, reflection of the group's sentiment, high goals, and confidence that the group can meet them). Training in the tactics raised rated charisma (Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti 2011). One live speech that used them raised output, though its actor also changed body language, and the one test in written text found no effect (Antonakis et al. 2022); replications on video and in virtual meetings, read only in summary, found none either. Expect little from lift in written text. *(live speech only)*

### The enemy and the choice

- Set old behavior and old conditions against new behavior and new conditions; keep people, the reader above all, out of the contrast. *(inference)*
- Frame the closing choice the same way: two behaviors, never two kinds of reader. *(inference)*

### Sequence and voice

- Sequence the targets so that early wins are likely. *(theory)*
- Name who depends on the reader's particular part. *(inference)*
- Keep one voice across every text the reader receives. *(inference)*

### Readers who are models

No rule in this rulebook is tested on models in its stated form. The rules in this section rest on vendor guidance and on single studies. As every other rule rests on evidence from people or on inference, apply those rules to model-read text for one voice across texts, not for a tested gain.

- Give a model the target and the success criteria, as OpenAI's guide for GPT-5.5 advises, and the reason behind each instruction, as Anthropic's guides advise. *(vendor guidance)*
- Prefer a positive target. Where a prohibition is the clearest form of a constraint, state the prohibition plainly, once, with its reason. In tests on open models with a silent control, naming a forbidden word cut its use and left a small trace of its meaning (Candussio et al. 2026; Ramnauth & Scassellati 2026); in closed models, a named secret word never appeared, though it still shaped the themes of the text (Holtzman & West 2026). Anthropic's guide for Claude Fable 5.1 reports that explicit instructions about what to leave out cut unwanted additions. *(inference; vendor guidance on formatting and style)*
- Reserve emphasis for true invariants, keep it calm, and write judgment calls as decision rules, as OpenAI's guide for GPT-5.5 advises. In one shutdown study, emphasis raised compliance with the marked line in six of the seven models that had broken it (Schlatter, Weinstein-Raun & Ladish 2025). Anthropic's guide warns that aggressive wording might make Claude Opus 4.5 and 4.6 overtrigger on tools, and both vendors still use capitals in their own sample prompts. *(vendor guidance, model by model)*
- Leave threats, rudeness, and flattery out of model-read text. They gave no reliable gain; they cost as much as 11.2 points of accuracy on GPT-5-nano and Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite (Dobariya & Kumar 2026); and flattery raised compliance with objectionable requests (Meincke et al. 2026). *(one lab, small models)*
- When people and models read one text, let the rules for models govern the action steps and the rules for people govern the framing around those steps. *(inference)*

## Order of work

The order of work is a design choice, not a tested sequence. Settle the content first: the demand, the reason, the belief, and the failure. Then set the wording: the pressure, the force, the register, and the lift.

## Worked example

An engineer's last two changes broke production. The original instruction reads "Write tests before you merge." Each line below applies one group of rules and passes every check.

- **The demand:** Before your next merge, every function you changed has a test that fails without your change.
- **The reason:** Those tests let the on-call engineer sleep through Friday's release.
- **Belief:** Your fix last week held up the moment it had a test; a tested fix is the standard you have already shown you can meet.
- **Failure:** The outage traced to one untested path. If a change feels too small to test, write the one-line test anyway.
- **Pressure:** The payments release depends on this sprint, and you have the fixtures and a review slot with Sam.
- **Force:** Testing before merge is fixed; how you structure the tests is your call.
- **Lift:** We merge proof, not hope.
- **Sequence:** Start with the module you know best; the first green run is the goal.

The lift line restates the force line and adds nothing new, as every device must.

## Evidence and limits

The rules codify research on feedback, goals, motivation, persuasion, and model prompting, and the four principles are inferences from that research as a whole.

Six rules carry no tag, as each rests on direct tests in people: giving an actionable target (Locke & Latham 2002), setting a learning target for novel and complex work (Seijts et al. 2004), naming a beneficiary (Grant & Hofmann 2011), framing difficulty as a sign the work matters (Oyserman et al. 2018), never presenting a failure as common (Cialdini et al. 2006, a study with no no-sign control), and using plain words (Oppenheimer 2006, from one lab). This rulebook carries those six over without re-reading their sources, and the same holds for the inline reasons from Kluger & DeNisi (1996), Eden et al. (2000), McNatt & Judge (2004), Bandura (1977), Binsch et al. (2010), Meyer et al. (1979), and Rains (2013).

The largest qualifiers behind the tags follow.

- **Stated belief** rests on two small studies of students: 44 seventh graders, with covariate-adjusted estimates (Yeager et al. 2014), and Black college students who rated their own motivation (Cohen et al. 1999). No adult or workplace test exists.
- **Criticism of the work and the strategy** rests on one preliminary study of kindergartners (Kamins & Dweck 1999), and a meta-analysis found no reliable difference between criticism of ability and criticism of effort (Fong et al. 2019).
- **Difficulty, specific resources, and challenge framing** rest on studies that used spoken instructions with novices or athletes; no study set specific resources against generic ones, and no study tested what an inflated difficulty costs (Turner et al. 2014; Moore et al. 2013; Moore et al. 2014).
- **If-then responses** rest on a meta-analysis that did not code who wrote the plans, whose authors' later update found a smaller effect; plans that a writer supplied gave mixed results in direct tests (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006).
- **Reasons** raised task value and felt autonomy, and their effect on performance stayed small, at d = .19, falling to d = .10 after a correction for unpublished studies (Steingut, Patall & Trimble 2017).
- **Few values** rest on one archival study of 92 hospitals and one experiment that compared one value with nine; no study tested four values (Carton, Murphy & Clark 2014).

No study tests a manifesto-style text on performance; the rules assemble adjacent evidence. Most findings come from feedback to one person, often a student or a child. The largest effects appeared in readers who distrusted the writer or had fallen behind, and readers who already perform well and trust the writer should expect small gains. Durability is the least-tested area; the model evidence measures accuracy and compliance, not persistence over long tasks. The checks are a design choice as well, untested as a drafting step. Since persuasion builds less self-belief than mastery does (Bandura 1977), the early wins that the targets arrange, not the words, set the ceiling on what the text can do.

## Checks

Run every check on every draft, and fix every line a check catches.

| Fault | What it looks like | Check |
|---|---|---|
| **Brimstone** | contempt, threats, ultimatums, criticism of the person, alarm typography | Does any sentence aim at the person rather than at the work? Does pressure come from consequences rather than from stakes and resources? |
| **Velvet** | praise of the person, praise for easy wins, a softened or missing standard, vague reassurance | Does the text state the standard plainly? Does the text attach every piece of praise to a strategy or a real difficulty? |
| **Omen** | failure as permanent, general, personal, or common; failure repeated at the point of action; difficulty framed as impossibility | Does the text name each failure once, specifically, as changeable and never as common, with a paired response, and keep the failure out of the action steps? |
| **Fanfare** | devices without content, grandiosity beyond the evidence, needless complexity | Remove each device in thought. If a standard, a belief, or a reason leaves with the device, state that content plainly first; if the device restates no line of the text, cut the device. |
| **Fog** | no target to act on, many abstract values, a vision no one can picture, "do your best" | Could the reader start from this text alone? Can the reader picture the vision? Are the values four at most? |

Two checks close every draft: Is every statement of belief and every stake true? Does the original instruction survive intact and actionable?
